The depreciation problem

A balance sheet is not a business model, and the break clauses are where the argument actually is.

Rowan Ives
ShareComment

Every few years the market decides that a balance sheet is a business model, and every few years it is disappointed. The current round is about capacity: whoever owns the machines owns the margin, and whoever rents them owns nothing but a contract.

That is the shape of the argument, and it is worth taking seriously before taking it apart. The people making it are not stupid, and the numbers they are quoting are real numbers from real filings.

Where the disagreement lives

Start with the depreciation schedule, because that is where the whole disagreement lives. A three-year life and a six-year life are the same asset and two completely different companies.

The chart below is the one everyone has been passing around. It is drawn from the segment disclosures, which are thin, and it should be read as an order of magnitude rather than as an estimate.

What it shows is a gap that has widened every quarter since the middle of last year. Some of that is mix. Most of it is not.

What maintenance costs

The second thing to look at is the maintenance line, which nobody discusses because it is boring and because it is buried. Boring and buried is usually where the answer is.

Here the disclosure is better, and the table below sets out what four operators reported on the same basis over the same period. The spread is wider than the averages suggest.

OperatorFY24FY25FY26e
Northwind4.1%4.4%4.6%
Calder Grid3.8%4.2%4.5%
Meridian DC4.0%4.3%4.4%
Halloway2.9%2.6%2.4%

Three of the four are converging. The fourth is not, and the fourth is the one whose multiple assumes it will.

None of this settles the question of whether the demand is real. It only settles the question of whether the accounting is telling you what you think it is telling you.

The assets are real. The contracts are shorter than the assets. That is the whole case.

The honest answer is that we will know in about eighteen months, when the first cohort of contracts comes up for renewal and the break clauses stop being theoretical.

Until then the sensible position is the boring one: the assets are real, the contracts are shorter than the assets, and the difference between those two facts is the entire investment case.
